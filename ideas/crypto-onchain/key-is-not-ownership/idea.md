## What we tried

While consolidating scattered funds we found an old wallet we had the private key for, holding a
small balance. The plan was to sweep it into the main account along with everything else.

## Why we thought it would work

We had the key. In every mental model of a crypto wallet, the key is ownership: whoever holds it
controls the funds. Nothing about the situation looked unusual.

## What actually happened

Before sweeping we read the account's transaction count and found dozens of transactions we had no
record of sending. The wallet had an activity history that was not ours. Whatever the balance was
doing there, it was being managed by somebody else who held the same key, and a sweep would have
been taking their funds rather than recovering ours.

We left it alone and wrote off the balance — a little over a dollar.

## Why it worked / why it failed

It failed in the useful direction: we discovered the ambiguity before acting on it rather than
after. The check that caught it was one RPC call.

The underlying mistake was treating "I can sign for this address" as equivalent to "this address is
mine". Those come apart whenever a key has an origin you do not fully control — generated by a
shared script, copied from a tutorial, restored from a backup somebody else also restored, or
derived from a seed used in more than one place. Exclusivity is an assumption, and it is checkable.

The stakes here were a rounding error. The same reasoning error at a larger balance is how people
take funds that are not theirs while sincerely believing otherwise.

## What you would need to change

Nothing would make this wallet recoverable; the key is shared and that is permanent. What changes is
the procedure: before treating any found key as a recovery, compare the address's nonce and transfer
history against your own records, and treat any activity you cannot account for as a hard stop.

## What to reuse

The one-line check. Read the transaction count, compare it to what you know you did, and stop if
they disagree. It costs a single call and it is the difference between recovering your own funds and
quietly spending somebody else's.
